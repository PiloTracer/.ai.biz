# BIZ-10: Advisory Patterns

Reusable judgment patterns for situational advice ("what should I do?"). Used by `@biz-advisory` during the reframe pass; each pattern is a repeatable move that turns an underpriced asset or a feared constraint into a stronger position. Generalized from real advisory cases (e.g. a laid-off architect + QA + developer team deciding between separate job hunts and going independent together).

## Questions to explore

1. What assets does the operator have that they are pricing as liabilities or ignoring entirely?
2. Is the stated question the real question? (Often "which job board?" hides "employment vs venture?")
3. What would an advisor who has seen 100 of these situations notice in the first five minutes?
4. Which pattern below changes the shape of the answer rather than its details?

## The patterns

### 1. Team-as-product
**When:** several people who already work well together face the market separately (layoff, agency collapse, project end).
**The move:** sell the *delivery unit*, not the individuals. "Three CVs" competes on salary; "a team that takes a product from mess → stable → shipping" competes on outcome. The team's shared history is the quality guarantee a newly assembled team can't offer.
**Failure mode:** treating the team as a prison — see pattern 8.

### 2. Productized entry offer
**When:** the operator needs a first client but asks for too much trust up front (long retainer, big project).
**The move:** a fixed-price, fixed-scope diagnostic (e.g. 30-day assessment + stabilization) that delivers standalone value *and* a roadmap. The roadmap execution is the upsell: "if you want us to execute this, we're available." Lowers the psychological barrier; prices the trust gap honestly.
**Failure mode:** pricing the diagnostic so low it attracts only tire-kickers, or so vague it can't be delivered in the window.

### 3. Trigger-based demand
**When:** choosing who to approach first.
**The move:** target buyers at a moment of pain that matches the offer — migrations (monolith → services, on-prem → cloud), AI-generated codebases that have become unmaintainable, post-layoff delivery gaps, failed agency relationships. "We'll migrate X without breaking Y" outsells "we build whatever you need."
**Failure mode:** inventing a trigger the market doesn't actually feel; verify with real postings/communities before committing.

### 4. Anchor channel
**When:** the operator has a former employer, ex-colleagues, vendors, or past clients.
**The move:** the first sales channel is the network that already trusts the work. The ask is not "do you know anyone hiring?" but "we're operating independently now — if you know a company struggling with <specific problem>, we'd love to talk." Check contractual / non-solicit constraints first.
**Failure mode:** leading with need instead of capability; skipping the legal check.

### 5. Complementor channel
**When:** a market looks crowded with competitors (agencies, consultancies, freelancers).
**The move:** sell *through* the competitors instead of against them — overloaded agencies need overflow architecture/QA/development capacity; they already have sales and contracts. Become their delivery extension.
**Failure mode:** accepting rates/terms that make the channel a trap; keep one direct-sales motion alive.

### 6. Dual-track hedging
**When:** the operator faces an either/or under financial pressure (jobs vs venture, niche A vs B).
**The move:** run both tracks in parallel with an explicit primary — e.g. individual employment applications (income hedge) + team venture (upside). Name the kill/scale trigger in advance ("if track B has 2 paying clients by day 60, it becomes primary").
**Failure mode:** splitting effort so thin both tracks fail; the hedge must be time-boxed.

### 7. Customers-before-company
**When:** the operator is about to spend the first month on an LLC, logo, website, domains, or a 40-page plan.
**The move:** stop. A one-page site and a credible LinkedIn presence are enough; revenue precedes infrastructure. Every week of polish before a first customer conversation is a week of untested assumptions.
**Failure mode:** using "no company yet" as an excuse for no outreach either — the sequence is conversations → first invoice → then formalize.

### 8. Modular configurations
**When:** a team (or a skill stack) is being sold as all-or-nothing.
**The move:** market the full unit as the default, but let clients buy subsets — architecture-only engagement, QA + developer for test modernization, the full squad for stabilization. The team is the default configuration, not a prison.
**Failure mode:** configurations so fragmented the coherent-unit story dissolves.

### 9. Control-layer positioning
**When:** the operator's edge includes fluency with a hyped tool (AI coding assistants today).
**The move:** never sell "we know tool X" — the tool is table stakes next quarter. Sell *governed acceleration*: the operator's judgment/QA as the control layer that makes the tool's speed safe. "AI helped you build fast; we make what you built maintainable."
**Failure mode:** tool-name marketing; also dismissing the tool instead of harnessing it.

### 10. Outcome-over-people
**When:** any services positioning.
**The move:** sell outcomes ("stable, shipping product in 90 days"), not resumes ("senior architect, 15 years"). Buyers purchase the change in their situation; headcount is the competitor's framing.
**Failure mode:** outcome promises the operator can't control — tie outcomes to scope and access.

## Output

- Which patterns apply to the operator's situation, and why
- The reframe sentence(s) — "don't sell X, sell Y" — ready to use
- The concrete offer structure implied (entry offer, configurations, ladder)
- Evidence to gather before committing (per pattern: what would prove the move works in this market)
